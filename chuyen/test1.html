A VIOLIN MAKER'S QUIET RISE

For most of her twenties, Vy Anh worked in near-total obscurity, repairing instruments in a small workshop her grandfather had left her. She had never studied under a master craftsman, and for years she was widely seen as punching above her weight whenever she attempted to build an instrument of her own rather than simply repair others'.

Her first few violins were, by her own admission, far from flawless. Rather than selling them quickly to recover her costs, she kept every rejected attempt in a back room, insisting she would rather lose money than let an imperfect instrument leave her workshop. It took nearly three years before she considered a single violin good enough to sell.

That patience eventually paid off. A touring musician who tried one of her instruments by chance recommended her to several colleagues, and within a year, Vy Anh had a waiting list stretching well into the following season. She remains at pains to stress, however, that the turning point was not luck but simply years of refusing to lower her own standards.

Today, her workshop is often cited as a benchmark for craftsmanship among younger instrument makers in the city, though she still works largely alone.
